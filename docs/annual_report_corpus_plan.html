About the source: the SEC, EDGAR and the filings

SEC is the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, the US federal agency that regulates the securities markets, created in 1934 after the 1929 crash. Its job is to protect investors and keep markets fair. A central rule is that public companies must disclose accurate financial information regularly. It is roughly the US counterpart of SEBI in India.

EDGAR stands for Electronic Data Gathering, Analysis, and Retrieval. It is the SEC's online system where companies submit their filings and anyone can read them for free at sec.gov. It holds filings back to the mid-1990s.

Who uploads the reports: the companies themselves, not the SEC. Every company with publicly traded US securities must file. Usually its finance and legal teams prepare the documents, often through a filing agent, a firm that formats and submits filings on companies' behalf. That is why so many URLs in this corpus contain 0001193125: that prefix is the filer ID of Donnelley Financial, a large filing agent. The SEC reviews some filings, but it doesn't write them or vouch for the numbers. The company and its auditor are responsible for those.

Why the SEC asks for a name and e-mail: EDGAR is free and public, but the SEC asks every automated program to identify itself in the request header, so it can contact whoever runs a script that misbehaves. It also limits traffic to 10 requests a second. That is why the notebooks have a SEC_CONTACT setting, and why the download plan below stays at 8 requests a second.

4. How to download them

  1. Use the plan, not a crawl. The list above is saved as tmp/corpus/plan.json; the download step reads it and appends to data/raw/financial_report_qa/edgar/pdf_manifest.jsonl in the existing format (document id edgar_ars_<TICKER>_<YEAR>, URL, accession, SHA-256, pages, bytes), so the current indexers read the new files unchanged.
  2. Follow SEC's rules. Send a User-Agent with a name and e-mail, stay under 10 requests a second (8 is safe), and back off on HTTP 429 or 503. At that rate 1,004 files take 15–30 minutes; the limit is bandwidth for 11.3 GB.
  3. Make it resumable. Skip files already present with a matching size, write to a .part file and rename when complete, record the SHA-256, and retry failures at the end.
  4. Check each file. Open it with PyMuPDF, record the page count, and flag files whose text layer is missing or broken, so they go to OCR or the VLM rather than text-only indexing.
  5. Where to keep them. Download once to Google Drive (MyDrive/rag_corpus/pdfs, 11.3 GB, so the free 15 GB is enough) and copy to the Colab runtime's local disk before processing; reading from Drive during indexing is slow.
  6. Stage it. Pilot one company per sector (about 50 PDFs) first to measure real speeds, then run each sector as a separate job so a disconnect loses at most one sector.

5. Colab GPU resources

The workload: 1,004 PDFs, about 148,382 pages (average pages per sector from the local PDFs). Recommendation

  1. Download all 1,004 PDFs to Drive (11.3 GB) and record pages, text quality and SHA-256 in the manifest.
  2. Index every page with ColPali into Qdrant (pooled vectors in RAM, full vectors on disk in float16), plus the text collection, on an L4. This is the only approach that covers all pages within budget.
  3. Use the VLM only where it adds the most: chart pages and pages without a usable text layer (about 28% of pages), batched with vLLM. It can also run at query time on the few pages ColPali retrieves, which needs no bulk transcription at all.
  4. Pilot about 50 PDFs, one company per sector, to replace these estimates with measured speeds before spending most of the budget.
